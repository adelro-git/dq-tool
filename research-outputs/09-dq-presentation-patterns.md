The 13 tools reviewed present data quality in much the same way. In every one, a health figure sits beside a coverage figure. Results that are not a verdict get named states of their own. Alerts are grouped into issues, and each issue states the measured value against the expected one. Scores are simple ratios with the method disclosed. Notifications go out as one thread per issue, with delivery tiered by importance. **Snowflake and Databricks both put a "% healthy tables" headline next to a "monitoring coverage" figure.** Databricks' docs screenshot reads "99.95% healthy" beside "Schemas Monitored 8 · 0.11% of all schemas". That is a deliberate guard against a high pass rate over a tiny monitored estate. The TDG Data Quality Monitor already holds several conventions that most vendors only partly meet: grey is never green, every number names its base, and there is no composite risk score. So the main job is not a redesign. It is to borrow three groups of things. **The first is coverage and "not run" states**, which need plan items A1 and A2. **The second is issue-level alert rows that show measured against expected**, which need A1 and A9. **The third is a Chat card and digest modelled on Elementary's alert layout and Bigeye's one-thread-per-issue behaviour.** Sixteen of the 39 panel changes below need no plan item at all. They are low-effort Chart.js and template work on data the marts already hold. None of the 39 depends on a Tier B ask. The evidence is uneven. **No Monte Carlo data-quality screenshot was found.** The Anomalo screens date from 2021. No current Great Expectations visuals exist publicly. The enterprise suites (Informatica, Collibra, Ataccama) and Dataplex are known only from their docs text. Treat the patterns attributed to those tools as described behaviour, not observed layout.

## Vendors converge on five presentation habits the Monitor can borrow cheaply

### Headline views show health and coverage together, with the window spelled out

Every home or executive view in the sample pairs a health figure with a coverage figure. Snowflake's account dashboard has two summary cards side by side. "Healthy tables" is the share of monitored tables with no open incidents. "Monitoring coverage" is the share of account tables with at least one active check ([Snowflake](https://docs.snowflake.com/en/user-guide/data-quality-centralized-dashboard)). Databricks prints the same pair, and its metastore view sorts catalogs so that "the most unhealthy catalogs appear first" ([Databricks](https://docs.databricks.com/aws/en/data-quality-monitoring/anomaly-detection/)). Sifflet's 2025 home page puts a **"65% Coverage" half-donut** next to its table-uptime figures, with unmonitored assets shown in **grey rather than red** ([Sifflet](https://docs.siffletdata.com/changelog/new-sifflet-dashboard-a-central-hub-for-your-datas-health)). Elementary shows a "Monitored tables" donut beside "Tables health". Its 2024 Cloud screenshot reads **59 monitored against 181 unmonitored out of 240 tables** ([Elementary Cloud image](https://res.cloudinary.com/diuctyblm/image/upload/v1701701243/elementary_cloud_data_observability_dashboard_dec_2024_oaos2y.png)). Making the gap visible on the same card as the pass rate is what stops a high pass rate from passing as trust.

Two smaller habits support the pairing. Soda writes the time window into every KPI label, as in "32 CHECKS FAILED (LAST 90 DAYS)". Its daily stacked bars show **grey placeholder bars on days with no runs**, so a silent day can be seen rather than vanishing ([Soda](https://docs.soda.io/manage-issues/organization-dashboard)). Monte Carlo's Data Reliability Dashboard leaves the current partial week or month out of trendlines by default, because partial periods skew them. Its docs also warn that the whole-range uptime number runs lower than the per-period chart next to it ([Monte Carlo](https://docs.getmontecarlo.com/docs/data-reliability-dashboard)). Monte Carlo's response measures are **medians**: median Time to Response (time to the first status update), median Time to "Fixed", and a Status Update Rate. Medians suit the Monitor's skewed "days to close" distribution better than means. Practitioner guidance points the same way for executive views: about six visuals at most, a freshness indicator, and targets and trends for context ([Mastech guide](https://digihub.mastechdigital.com/Fabric_Assets/Microsoft%20Fabric%20Asset%20Library/10_Data_Quality/MS_Fabric_Quality_Monitoring_Dashboards.docx)). Detail should sit one click below, in four levels: summary scores, then decompositions, then error reports, then individual records ([Data Quality Pro](https://www.dataqualitypro.com/blog/how-to-create-data-quality-scorecards)).

### Alert feeds are grouped issues with plain-English titles and a visible impact count

The feed designs that were seen share one layout. A left-hand facet panel lists counts per value. There is a search bar and a sort control. Each row pairs a title and an asset breadcrumb with status and severity chips, an owner, and a relative timestamp. Bigeye writes its titles as **"{metric type} – {plain-English problem} on {column}"**, for example "Null (#) - Too many null values on ORDER_ID". Each row carries an **inline sparkline with a grey expected band and a red mark on the anomalous point**. Later out-of-bounds runs are threaded into the same issue rather than opening new ones ([Bigeye](https://docs.bigeye.com/docs/view-issues); [Bigeye Issues](https://docs.bigeye.com/docs/issues)). Sifflet's incident cards add a downstream-impact count, "5 Compromised dashboards". Its status chips use **shape as well as colour**: an up-arrow for Open, a right-arrow for In progress, a check for Closed, and a stack of one to three chevrons for severity. The states can still be told apart in greyscale ([Sifflet Incidents](https://docs.siffletdata.com/docs/incidents)). Monte Carlo groups table-monitor alerts from the same monitor and schema within a five-hour window ([Interacting with Alerts](https://docs.getmontecarlo.com/docs/interacting-with-incidents)). Databricks ranks unhealthy tables by an Impact column (High, Medium, Low), derived from downstream lineage and query volume, and links Root Cause to the failing upstream job ([Databricks](https://docs.databricks.com/aws/en/data-quality-monitoring/anomaly-detection/)).

Incident detail pages now open with prose. Sifflet's May 2026 screenshot leads with an AI-written overview that states the measured value against the expected range. Its example reads: SUM "was 10,501, which is lower than the expected range of 10,713 to 16,515". The page also has tabs for Monitors, Downstream Impacts, Lineage and Notifications, and an activity feed that logs status changes and ServiceNow or Jira events ([Sifflet Incidents](https://docs.siffletdata.com/docs/incidents)). The Monitor's Gemini headliners already fill this slot. What Sifflet adds is the discipline of putting the two numbers, observed and expected, in the first sentence. Soda's incident table is the smallest useful record: severity, ID and title, status, lead, and the reported time **in both relative and absolute form**, "about 1 month ago (Jan 19, 6:26 AM)" ([Soda](https://docs.soda.io/manage-issues/dataset-dashboard)).

### Table health pages separate the measured value from the verdict and name every non-verdict state

Snowflake's per-object tab groups checks by dimension. Each group is a card with one roll-up pill, "All checks passed" or "4 checks failed". Inside the card, the **raw metric value, a trend sparkline and the "x/y passed" verdict sit in separate columns**. In one screenshot a null count of 6,653,934 still reads as passed, because the expectation sets the verdict, not the size of the number ([Classmethod](https://dev.classmethod.jp/articles/snowsight-data-quality-tab/)). Databricks gives the most complete status vocabulary: **Healthy, Unhealthy, Training, Error, Excluded, Not enabled**. Its Quality tab shows "No data yet. Only high impact tables are monitored for null value anomalies." for a section it does not measure ([Databricks](https://docs.databricks.com/aws/en/data-quality-monitoring/anomaly-detection/)). Snowflake's anomaly models report TRAINING_IN_PROGRESS until they have at least two weeks of data, and Snowflake recommends 60 days ([Snowflake anomaly docs](https://docs.snowflake.com/en/user-guide/data-quality-anomaly)). These are the vendor equivalents of the Monitor's grey-never-green and Unverified conventions. They confirm the conventions rather than challenge them.

Anomaly charts were thin in the evidence. The Databricks Quality tab is the only full chart seen. Its completeness view draws a **green observed line inside a light-blue predicted band, with a "Last evaluated" dot** and a footer reading "Predicted daily volume 14,538 rows". Its freshness view draws past commits as ticks against a blue predicted commit window and a yellow stale window. Soda's Metric Monitor cards take a lighter approach. Each card shows a big current value, an "Expected: 7.95k – 8.15k rows" line and a dotted sparkline with a red marker ([Soda](https://docs.soda.io/manage-issues/dataset-dashboard)). Anomalo's root-cause chart ranks `column = value` segments by the share of bad rows each one explains, compared with its share of good rows ([Anomalo](https://www.anomalo.com/blog/root-causing-data-failures/)). The Monitor's Anomaly Monitor already draws an actual line, a forecast and a translucent band with amber dots, which is ahead of most of the sample. What it lacks is the text line with the expected range, the last-evaluated marker, and an explicit Training state.

### Scores are ratios, the method is shown, and bands come from a target and a threshold

No observability vendor in the sample leads with a dimension-weighted composite. Monte Carlo, Sifflet, Snowflake and Databricks lead with **uptime or % healthy tables**: a binary verdict per table, counted. The scorecard-heavy suites differ in their maths, which is why the method has to be disclosed. Informatica CDGC averages child scores; its worked example is columns at 94, 86, 78 and 82 giving a table score of 85%. It bands each rule with a **target and a threshold**: Good at or above target, Acceptable between the two, Not Acceptable below threshold ([Trailhead](https://trailhead.salesforce.com/content/learn/modules/data-asset-quality-and-classification-in-informatica-cdgc/review-and-interpret-data-quality-results)). Collibra uses passing monitors divided by active monitors. It counts "Learning" monitors as passing, and its docs warn that a passing score does not guarantee there are no issues ([Collibra](https://productresources.collibra.com/docs/collibra/latest/Content/UnifiedDataQuality/co_about-data-quality-scores.htm)). Ataccama uses record-level all-rules-pass, so dimensions passing at 25% and 50% give an **overall score of 25%, not 37.5%** ([Ataccama](https://docs.ataccama.com/one/latest/data-quality/run-dq-evaluation.html)). Dataplex reports a dimension score only when at least one rule is tagged with that dimension ([Dataplex](https://docs.cloud.google.com/dataplex/docs/reference/rest/v1/DataQualityResult)). That is the same logic as the Monitor's "No score" for out-of-scope areas. Acceldata shows the aggregation method in a tooltip on the score. Its docs also state that changing the method **does not recalculate historical scores** ([Acceldata](https://documentation.acceldata.io/docs/acceldata/adoc/documentation/score-aggregation-methodology)). For the DQI, that is the presentation side of plan item A8, verdict versioning.

Soda shows the main risk in pass-ratio scores. Its dataset-page health score **excludes checks that have never been evaluated** ([Soda](https://docs.soda.io/manage-issues/dataset-dashboard)). That is exactly the survivorship mechanism behind the Gold DQI sitting flat at 100% since February (weakness W5 in the robustness plan). Practitioner guidance is blunt about enterprise averages: "A simple aggregate score for the entire database is usually rather meaningless", and the most valuable scores "measure data fitness for various business uses" ([Data Quality Pro](https://www.dataqualitypro.com/blog/how-to-create-data-quality-scorecards)). The Monitor's per-report health cards already follow that advice.

### Notifications are one living thread per issue, with delivery tiered by severity

Bigeye's Slack behaviour is the best-documented anti-noise pattern. **Each issue gets one thread. The root message is edited to show the current status, and lifecycle events post as replies** ([Bigeye Slack](https://docs.bigeye.com/docs/slack)). Elementary's Slack alert is the only message body seen in full (2024-02-12). It has a red left border and a headline in the form `Failure: "not_null" test failed on <db.schema.table.column>`. Below that come a meta line with test, status and timestamp; a field grid with table, column, tags, owners and subscribers as @-mentions, and description; a Result section with a plain-English message ("Got 2 results, configured to fail if != 0"), sample rows and the test SQL; and a Configuration section ([Elementary](https://docs.elementary-data.com/oss/guides/alerts/elementary-alerts)). Snowflake throttles notifications with `cooldown_hours`, and its docs example uses 4 hours ([Snowflake notifications](https://docs.snowflake.com/en/user-guide/data-quality-notifications)). Monte Carlo sends alerts about **monitors that failed to run immediately**, whatever the routing ([Notifications](https://docs.getmontecarlo.com/docs/notifications-v2)). Its alert-fatigue guidance is real-time delivery for critical tables and digests for the rest ([Monte Carlo blog](https://montecarlo.ai/blog-alert-fatigue-monitoring-strategy)). These vendors also let users acknowledge or close issues from Slack. The Monitor should not copy that part, for the reasons given in the "patterns to avoid" section.

They are mostly label, sort, chip and empty-state changes, and together they deliver most of the "read it at a glance" gain. **A1 and A2 unlock the next tier**, nine rows that turn binary pass/fail into measured-vs-expected and make survivorship visible: M1, M9, M21, M25, M26, M27, M32, M34 and M36. This is the strongest presentation-side argument for doing A1 and A2 in weeks 2–6, as the robustness plan proposes. The payload is the stack's real constraint. Each sparkline in the feed or in the "by table" lens adds series data to an already 1.5 MB page. Small multiples (M13) and history strips (M9) should be fetched per lens through a separate cached Apps Script call, not embedded in the first load. That is an inference about the stack, to confirm against the code.

## Notification template: one living Chat card per issue, one digest per day

The template below combines Elementary's message anatomy with Bigeye's thread-per-issue behaviour. Snowflake contributes the cooldown, and Monte Carlo the failed-to-run override and the real-time/digest tiering. It is scoped so that the Monitor stays an observer. **Cards carry links to ServiceNow, Clairvoyance and the Monitor, and no buttons that change state.** Once Clairvoyance becomes the alerting tool of record, the real-time card should either move into Clairvoyance or fall back to issues that only the Monitor can see: verdict changes, grouped issues, silent checks, and stale sources. The digest stays with the Monitor in either case.

Routing follows four rules. An issue tagged to a report, or on a Gold-layer table, posts a card in real time. Everything else waits for the digest. A check or feed that **failed to run or went silent** always posts in real time, because an unmeasured state is the one most easily mistaken for health. A **cooldown of 4 hours per issue** suppresses repeat cards, and later alerts on the same issue become thread replies. The 4-hour figure is Snowflake's docs example, a starting point and not a recommendation. Google Chat incoming webhooks support threading by thread key. Editing the root message in place, Bigeye's key behaviour, may require the Chat API with app credentials rather than a plain webhook. That is an inference to verify in the existing Chat-card code before relying on it.

## Patterns that would break the Monitor's conventions

Several vendor patterns are attractive but clash with rules the Monitor has deliberately set, or with Globe's privacy and system-of-record boundaries. Each row below names the pattern, where it was seen, and what to do instead.

| Pattern | Where seen | Why it does not fit | Do instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| One enterprise-wide DQ score or average | Informatica CDGC averaging; the DEV Community "enterprise tier" scorecard ([DEV](https://dev.to/kapusto/how-to-build-an-effective-data-quality-scorecard-66)) | Hides which report is failing, and conflicts with the no-composite rule; "usually rather meaningless" ([Data Quality Pro](https://www.dataqualitypro.com/blog/how-to-create-data-quality-scorecards)) | Per-report RAG cards and separate Silver and Gold composites (M1, M25) |
| Weighted or deduction-based composite scores | DataKitchen TestGen weights up to 10× ([TestGen](https://docs.datakitchen.io/testgen/quality-scores/)); legacy Collibra "deduct from 100" | A composite risk score by another name; the weights cannot be audited by readers | Counts with bases, and non-compensating gates (M10) |
| Hidden priority score on hover | Bigeye priority from popularity, issue count and distance from threshold ([Bigeye](https://docs.bigeye.com/docs/view-issues)) | An opaque composite deciding sort order | Sort by named, separate fields: report impact, then age (M20, M21) |
| Counting "Learning" or never-evaluated checks as passing, or dropping them from the base | Collibra counts Learning as passing; Soda excludes never-run checks | Turns unmeasured into green, the Gold-100% failure mode | `NOT_RUN` stays in the base as grey (M24, M25) |
| AI suggestion cards with Accept buttons, or auto-applied tuning | Sifflet "Lower model sensitivity 50 → 25 [Accept]"; Monte Carlo Tuning Agent auto-apply ([Tuning agent](https://docs.getmontecarlo.com/docs/tuning-agent)) | Bypasses human approval of thresholds and lets AI into the detection path, against robustness plan §4 | Gemini drafts proposals into a review queue that analysts approve through `checks.yml` |
| Triage buttons in the dashboard or chat (Acknowledge, Assign to me, Not an issue, Close) | Databricks "Not an issue" marks a table Healthy for 7 days; Bigeye acknowledge/close from Slack | Makes the Monitor a second ticketing system next to ServiceNow, and "Not an issue → Healthy" overrides a verdict with a click | Links out to ServiceNow and Clairvoyance; verdicts stay with the versioned classifier (A8) |
| Failing-row samples in the app or the alert | Anomalo bad-row viewer; Elementary sample rows in Slack; Snowflake "View failed records" | Subscriber-level rows leave the warehouse; breaks the aggregates-first rule in the earlier research | Counts of failed records (A1) plus a link to the capped in-warehouse query (MRD pattern) |
| Unlabelled whole-range uptime next to period charts | Monte Carlo's own docs warn the two disagree | Readers see two numbers that seem to contradict each other | Label windows on every tile (M2) |
| A frequently reshuffled interface | Monte Carlo reviewers: "a lot is going on and I'm not sure where to look", "UI keeps changing" ([G2](https://www.g2.com/products/monte-carlo/reviews?page=4)) | Regular readers lose their place | Keep panel order and positions stable; add within panels |

Bigeye's priority score and Databricks' Impact column both mix severity with reach. The Monitor's Reports Impact mapping already gives reach as a named count. Keeping reach and severity as two visible columns, rather than one blended rank, keeps faith with the no-composite rule without giving up the sort order.

## Conclusion

The research reverses the usual build-versus-buy anxiety on presentation. On the hardest presentation questions, the Monitor is ahead of the commercial tools. Several of them count learning or never-run checks as passing. Several hide composite priority scores. Several offer one-click triage that overrides verdicts. The Monitor's rules forbid all three. What the vendors do better is plumbing that shows up on screen: they always know how many checks *should* have run, and they always carry a measured number next to the verdict. So the presentation roadmap and the robustness plan are the same roadmap seen from two ends. A1 and A2 are not back-end hygiene that the UI waits on. They are the single change that would let the Executive Dashboard, the DQI Report and Governance & Risk show the coverage-beside-health pairing every vendor treats as table stakes.

The second implication is about the Monitor's role once Clairvoyance and ServiceNow mature. The patterns worth borrowing, such as grouped issues, impact counts, explicit unmeasured states and a calm daily digest, are about *interpreting* signals, not raising or working them. The ones to refuse, such as Accept buttons, close-from-chat and auto-tuning, are about *acting*. That split gives a durable boundary. The Monitor should become the place that explains, with bases and grey states intact, what the alerting and ticketing tools did. It should not try to become those tools.
